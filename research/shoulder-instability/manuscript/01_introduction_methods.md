## Introduction

Traumatic anterior glenohumeral dislocation mainly affects young, active patients, and recurrence after a first event is common.^1,2^ Arthroscopic Bankart repair has become the predominant stabilization procedure.^3^ A meta-analysis of 11,038 isolated arthroscopic Bankart repairs reported pooled recurrence of 13.3%,^4^ rising to 17% in contact athletes^5^ and to 26.0% when the Instability Severity Index Score (ISIS) was 4 or higher.^6,7^ Failures also continue to accrue after the second year.^8^

Recurrence is now understood to depend less on the labral lesion than on bipolar bone loss. Yamamoto et al described the glenoid track,^9^ and Di Giacomo et al turned it into a treatment paradigm in which a Hill-Sachs lesion lying outside the track (off-track) engages and must be addressed.^10,11^ After isolated Bankart repair, off-track shoulders failed in 75% compared with 8% of on-track shoulders.^12^ The "critical" threshold has also fallen: function after isolated Bankart repair deteriorated above 13.5% glenoid bone loss even without redislocation,^13,14^ and on-track lesions with a distance to dislocation under 8 mm behaved like off-track lesions.^15^ Procedure choice is therefore decided by glenoid bone loss and track status together, measured on 3-dimensional computed tomography (3D-CT), and is least settled in the subcritical range of roughly 13.5% to 20% or 25%.^16^

Arthroscopic infraspinatus remplissage addresses the humeral side. In a double-blind multicenter randomized trial of patients with an engaging Hill-Sachs lesion and less than 15% glenoid bone loss, adding remplissage to Bankart repair reduced recurrent dislocation at 2 years from 18% to 4%,^17^ and the gap persisted at a mean of about 4 years (22% vs 8%).^18^ Meta-analyses estimate 4- to 9-fold higher odds of recurrence without remplissage,^19-21^ at a cost of about 1° to 6° of external rotation.^20,22^ The benefit extends to high-risk on-track lesions.^23^ Compared with the Latarjet procedure, pooled cohort data show similar recurrence and fewer complications after Bankart repair with remplissage (1.6% vs 7.7%),^24,25^ but most of these reviews were rated critically low in quality.^26^ The off-track shoulder with subcritical bone loss remains contested: with more than 15% glenoid bone loss, one series reported 28.6% recurrence after remplissage versus 6.1% after Latarjet,^27^ whereas another found no redislocation in either arm at a mean bone loss near 25%.^28^ The STABLE randomized trial (10% to 20% bone loss) has not reported.^29^

The modified Mason-Allen stitch was developed for rotator cuff repair, where it resisted pull-through better than simple stitches in an ovine model,^30^ although an arthroscopic anchor-based version was not stronger than a mattress stitch.^31^ Park et al adapted it to the labrum as the Bankart Repair Using Mason-Allen (BRUMA) technique and, in a randomized trial against a vertical simple stitch, found no difference in labral height, redislocation (4.9% vs 4.4%), or functional scores.^32^ The same group later reported BRUMA for small bony Bankart lesions: in 32 shoulders, recurrence was 6.3% and union 93.8% at a mean of 21.5 months, with remplissage added in 15 shoulders.^33^ Cohorts comparing Mason-Allen-type or hybrid constructs with simple stitches have found similar recurrence,^34,35^ and knotted and knotless repairs perform equivalently in pooled analyses.^36,37^ No study has compared a Mason-Allen-configured repair with a knotless repair, included both soft-tissue and bony Bankart lesions, stratified outcomes by 3D-CT bone loss and glenoid track, or asked whether such a strategy avoids the need for a bone block. All BRUMA data come from East Asian centers, and Indian reports of Bankart repair with remplissage are single-center series.^38,39^

From late 2018 the senior author changed from knotless Bankart repair with selective remplissage to BRUMA repair with remplissage for every Hill-Sachs lesion, and has since rarely used a bone block. The purpose of this study was to compare these 2 whole strategies, not the strength of the stitch, using time to recurrent instability as the primary outcome and the Western Ontario Shoulder Instability Index (WOSI) as the co-primary outcome at a minimum of 2 years, with results stratified by 3D-CT glenoid bone loss and glenoid-track status. We hypothesized that the BRUMA strategy would be associated with lower recurrence than the knotless strategy with WOSI that was not worse, and that this would hold in shoulders with 13.5% to 25% glenoid bone loss and off-track or near-track lesions, in which a bone block would conventionally be considered.

## Methods

### Study Design and Ethics

This was a single-center retrospective comparative cohort study with prospective completion of follow-up. It was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of [PLACEHOLDER: institution; committee registered with the Department of Health Research] (approval number [PLACEHOLDER], dated [PLACEHOLDER]) and registered with the Clinical Trials Registry-India (CTRI/[PLACEHOLDER]) before any research data extraction or patient contact. The committee waived consent for review of existing records. Patients contacted for follow-up gave informed consent, and patients younger than 18 years at recall gave assent with parental consent. Follow-up questionnaires collected as routine care were used for research only after approval. The study was reported in line with the STROBE statement for cohort studies.^40^

### Patients

Consecutive arthroscopic stabilizations performed by the senior author between August 2014 and October 7, 2024, were identified from the practice's electronic records: the operating theatre register, operative notes, implant records, outpatient follow-up notes, and every clinical spreadsheet imported into the record system. The cut-off date allowed a minimum of 2 years of follow-up at data lock in October 2026. Of 393 patients with shoulder instability in these records, those meeting the following criteria were included.

Inclusion criteria were age 14 years or older at surgery; traumatic anterior instability, either recurrent or a first-time dislocation in a high-risk patient (flagged and analyzed as a covariate); primary arthroscopic stabilization with a knotless Bankart repair or a BRUMA repair, with or without remplissage; and a minimum follow-up of 24 months, or recurrence before 24 months. Patients with early failure were retained.

Exclusion criteria were concomitant rotator cuff repair; posterior or combined anterior-posterior (pan-labral) repair; paralabral cyst decompression; revision stabilization (analyzed separately where numbers allowed); instability related to epilepsy; and primary bone block (Latarjet) procedures. Patients treated with a primary Latarjet during the same period were reported descriptively as a bone-block reference group. Excluded patients were flagged in the dataset rather than deleted, so that the flow diagram could be reproduced.

**Exposure classification.** Groups were assigned by transparent rules applied to the records, in the following order.

1. *Technique.* The surgeon's own list of operations by Bankart technique was the primary source. Where a patient was not on the list, operations before October 20, 2018, were classified as knotless and those after November 26, 2019, as BRUMA. Operations within the overlap period, when both techniques were in use, were classified only from the surgeon's list.
2. *Remplissage (implant rule).* The standard construct used 2 glenoid anchors for the Bankart repair, a third anchor for remplissage, and a fourth for a superior labrum anterior-posterior (SLAP) repair when present. A procedure recorded as a Bankart repair whose implant record showed an additional anchor, with no SLAP or other repair to account for it, was classified as Bankart repair with remplissage.
3. *Surgeon confirmation.* The surgeon confirmed that every BRUMA Bankart repair performed in 2023 included a remplissage, and these procedures were classified accordingly.

The indication for remplissage differed by era. In the knotless era it was selective, and shallow Hill-Sachs lesions were not filled [PLACEHOLDER: surgeon to state the knotless-era indication precisely]. In the BRUMA era, remplissage was performed for every Hill-Sachs lesion. This difference is part of the comparison and was handled in the analysis.

By these rules, 151 patients were eligible for the main study: 64 in the knotless group (51 Bankart repair alone, 13 with remplissage) and 87 in the BRUMA group (16 Bankart repair alone, 71 with remplissage). Eleven primary Latarjet procedures formed the reference group. The numbers analyzed after follow-up are given in Results.

### Imaging Assessment

Preoperative glenoid and humeral bone loss was measured on 3D-CT retrieved from the picture archiving and communication system [PLACEHOLDER: scanner, slice thickness, reconstruction software]. Patients without a preoperative 3D-CT were [PLACEHOLDER: surgeon to state handling, e.g. measured on 2D-CT or MRI and flagged, or excluded from imaging-based analyses]; the modality used was recorded for each patient.

1. *Glenoid bone loss.* On an en-face 3D-CT view of the glenoid with the humeral head subtracted, a best-fit circle was drawn on the inferior glenoid.^42^ Its diameter (D) and the width of the anterior defect (d) were measured, and glenoid bone loss (GBL) was calculated as d/D × 100. GBL was grouped as less than 13.5%, 13.5% to 20%, and more than 20%.^13^
2. *Hill-Sachs lesion.* The width of the lesion and the bone bridge between the rotator cuff footprint and the medial margin of the lesion were measured, and the Hill-Sachs interval (HSI) was their sum. Lesion depth was also recorded [PLACEHOLDER: view and method for humeral measurements].
3. *Glenoid track.* The glenoid track (GT) was calculated as 0.83 × D − d.^10^ A lesion was off-track when HSI exceeded GT. Distance to dislocation (DTD) was calculated as GT − HSI, and an on-track lesion with DTD less than 8 mm was classified as near-track.^15^
4. *Bony Bankart lesion.* When present, fragment size was expressed as a percentage of D. Because the BRUMA technique can reduce small fragments indirectly,^33^ fragment union was recorded where postoperative imaging was available.

Measurements were made by 2 independent observers [PLACEHOLDER: identities and experience], blinded to treatment group and outcome where possible [PLACEHOLDER: state how blinding was achieved]. For reliability, both observers measured a random subset of 30 patients, and observer 1 repeated these measurements at least 2 weeks later. Interobserver and intraobserver reliability for D, d, Hill-Sachs width, and bone bridge were reported as intraclass correlation coefficients, ICC(2,1), with 95% CIs.

### Outcomes and Follow-up

*Primary outcome.* The primary outcome was time to recurrent instability, defined as a redislocation (requiring reduction or documented on imaging) or a patient-reported subluxation, whichever occurred first, measured in months from surgery.

*Co-primary outcome.* The co-primary outcome was the WOSI^43^ at 2 years or later. The WOSI raw total (0 to 2100, lower is better) was converted to a percentage of normal, 100 × (2100 − raw)/2100, so that 100 indicates no symptoms. for comparison with the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of approximately 13 to 18 points.^44,45^ Preoperative WOSI was recorded where available.

*Secondary outcomes.* Secondary outcomes were redislocation alone; revision surgery for instability, including any subsequent bone block procedure (the bone-block-avoidance endpoint); positive apprehension test at final follow-up; and composite clinical failure, defined as recurrence, revision for instability, or positive apprehension at final follow-up (on examination, or patient-reported feeling that the shoulder might give way or avoidance of the provoking position). Patient-reported outcomes were the Oxford Instability Score (OIS)^46^, the Rowe score^47^, the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score^48^, the Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation (SANE)^49^, and pain on a 0 to 10 visual analog scale. The OIS was reported on the current 0 to 48 scale, with higher scores indicating better function. Values from the surgeon's historical score sheets had been recorded on a 12 to 60 scale, also with higher scores better (confirmed against scored forms), and were converted by subtracting 12; when both a scored electronic form and a sheet value were available, the form score was used. External rotation in 90° of abduction (ER2) was measured with a goniometer on both sides, and the side-to-side deficit was calculated as the main cost of remplissage. Other outcomes were return to any sport and to the same level, time to return, complications graded by the Clavien-Dindo classification^50^, reoperations, and glenohumeral arthropathy on final radiographs graded by Samilson-Prieto^51^.

*Covariates.* Recorded covariates included age, sex, sport and level of participation, contact or collision sport, ISIS,^7^ number of preoperative dislocations, hyperlaxity, lesion type, GBL, track status, Hill-Sachs presence, remplissage, and number of anchors, following published recommendations for risk-factor reporting.^52^

*Follow-up.* Follow-up data missing from the records were completed prospectively. Patients were contacted by clinic recall or by structured telephone or video contact, and questionnaires were self-administered through secure links sent by message [PLACEHOLDER: platform used]. The date and mode of each questionnaire (clinic, telephone, video, or online) were recorded. Rowe score, range of motion, apprehension testing, and radiographs required a clinic examination [PLACEHOLDER: examiner and whether blinded to group]. The minimum follow-up was 2 years. Follow-up duration was measured from surgery to the last contact at which recurrence status was known. Patients who could not be contacted for final data were classified as lost to follow-up.

### Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized by group [PLACEHOLDER: summary statistics, e.g. mean and SD or median and IQR; balance diagnostics, e.g. standardized mean differences before and after weighting].

*Primary analysis.* Recurrence-free survival was estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method and compared between groups with the log-rank test. Hazard ratios were estimated with Cox proportional hazards regression [PLACEHOLDER: method of checking proportional hazards]. Because few recurrences were expected, confounding was addressed with inverse probability of treatment weighting, using a propensity score built from age younger than 20 years, contact or collision sport, ISIS, GBL band, and track status, rather than by entering many covariates into the model [PLACEHOLDER: weight stabilization or truncation, if used]. Because the knotless group had longer follow-up and late failures accumulate,^8,18^ recurrence was also reported for both groups at a fixed 2-year horizon.

*Remplissage.* Remplissage was part of the BRUMA strategy (71 of 87 patients) and was used selectively in the knotless group (13 of 64). Three analyses were therefore reported: the whole-strategy comparison (primary); results stratified by Bankart repair alone and Bankart repair with remplissage; and a model including remplissage and Hill-Sachs status.

*Era and learning curve.* Two sensitivity analyses were performed: one restricted to the overlap period (October 20, 2018, to November 26, 2019), when both techniques were in use, and one excluding the first 10 cases of each technique.

*WOSI and other continuous outcomes.* Final WOSI was compared by analysis of covariance adjusted for preoperative WOSI where available, and the proportion of patients reaching the MCID was compared between groups. Final ER2 was compared by analysis of covariance adjusted for preoperative ER2, and the side-to-side deficit was reported.

*Subgroups.* Recurrence was reported by GBL band (less than 13.5%, 13.5% to 20%, more than 20%) and track status (on-track, near-track, off-track) for each technique, with 95% CIs, alongside published benchmarks for Latarjet and for Bankart repair with remplissage.^17,27,28^ The key subgroup was patients with 13.5% to 25% GBL and off-track or near-track lesions, in whom a bone block would usually be chosen.

*Missing data.* Primary analyses used complete cases. Missing covariates were also handled by multiple imputation by chained equations with 20 imputed datasets. Patients lost to follow-up were compared with those retained.

*Power.* For WOSI, assuming an MCID of 12.8 points on the 0 to 100 scale^44^ and an SD of 20 to 25 points, 39 to 60 patients per group were needed (two-sided α = .05, power 80%). With 64 and 87 eligible patients, this was achievable if about 75% were reached and the SD was close to 20. For recurrence, detecting the trial effect of 18% versus 4%^17^ required about 78 patients per group, which was borderline, and detecting 15% versus 5% required about 141 per group, which was not available. Recurrence was therefore reported with 95% CIs and an adjusted hazard ratio, and WOSI carried the formal comparison.

All tests were two-sided with α = .05. Analyses were performed in R version [PLACEHOLDER] (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) [PLACEHOLDER: packages, e.g. survival, WeightIt, mice].
